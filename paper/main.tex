\begin{abstract}
Given the masks of the objects of interest in one camera of a static
multicamera rig at one instant, the task is to produce each object's mask in
every other camera and subsequent frame with no calibration, category label,
or per-scene optimization: a spatial-intelligence question on real-world
video, whether a segmentation foundation model keeps a geometrically and
temporally consistent notion of an object across a rig without a 3D model. On
the MUVOD rig benchmark, a two-stage pipeline on a promptable foundation model
reaches \OursBasic\ J\&F (basic) and \OursComplete\ (complete) against the
published \XMemBasic/\XMemComplete, a paired gain of \DeltaBasic. We decompose
the margin: \gainFoundation\ points separate the published baseline from the
same two-stage structure with the foundation model as tracker, which also
carries implementation and evaluation differences; \gainOurs\ come from our
design: cross-view memory, raw frame-0 seeding, and zoom-gated crop
retracking. Rerunning the tracker from ground-truth seeds recovers
\gtSeedHeadlineDelta\ of the remaining \totalGap\ points. Five preregistered
seed-repair attempts fail their gates, and a ground-truth-free vote over their
candidates repairs some seeds but shows no net gain, because \nFzero\
nonreference seeds need repair against \nNzero\ sound ones---a \asymmetry\
ratio under which, at the vote's gains and losses, a selector wins only by
damaging sound seeds \breakEvenTimes\ times less often than it repairs
failing ones. On a \plenCams-camera studio ring, starting the pipeline from
the text prompt ``person'' on one camera gives a first-frame mean \Jscore\
of \plenJtxt\ on the other cameras against \plenJgt\ from ground-truth
seeding, without an external detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{static} multicamera rig---a light-field array, a volumetric
capture studio, an instrumented sports arena---observes one scene from many
viewpoints at the same instant. When a person marks one object in one of
those cameras, the natural expectation is that the mark should suffice: the
same object should be found in every other camera, and followed through time
in each. This paper is about that expectation. The input is the set of binary masks
of the objects of interest in one camera at one instant, one mask per object;
the output is the mask of each object in every camera and every subsequent
frame, the objects being propagated jointly. No camera calibration is provided,
no category label is attached to the object, and no per-scene training or
reconstruction is permitted.

The task sits between two well-studied problems and belongs to neither. It is
not video object segmentation: the cameras are not a temporal sequence, their
baselines are wide, and their index order carries no guarantee of visual
adjacency. It is not 3D segmentation in the sense of the neural radiance field
(NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) literature: those methods assume
calibrated poses and a pre-trained scene representation, and the best of them
now report about 92 mean intersection over union (mIoU) on the NVOS benchmark
\cite{sagonline,flashsplat,ludvig}. Treating the views as a video and
propagating with a memory-based tracker is the obvious alternative, and its
failure has been quantified: the segment-correspondence area under the
precision--recall curve (AUPRC) of Segment Anything Model (SAM)~2 video
propagation \cite{sam2} on ScanNet++ falls from 61.9 at
$0^{\circ}$--$45^{\circ}$ view difference to 17.0 at
$135^{\circ}$--$180^{\circ}$ \cite{segmast3r}. The 2026 generation of
uncalibrated promptable methods \cite{mvsam,am3,g2tam} learns its multiview
head on indoor scans (G$^{2}$TAM), on synthetic and indoor scans plus video
(3AM), or on single-view SA-1B masks alone (MV-SAM); none has been evaluated on rigs, on environment objects such as wall
and floor fragments, or on fisheye cameras.

Only one public benchmark poses the rig problem directly. MUVOD \cite{muvod}
provides \nScenes\ scenes captured by \nCamMin--\nCamMax\ synchronised
cameras, with \nInstances\ object instances over \nCategories\ categories,
four hemispherical fisheye rigs among them, and a published baseline that
applies a video tracker along both the spatial and the temporal axis. At the
time of writing no method other than that baseline has been reported on its
video benchmark, and no later paper has evaluated on it. This has a
consequence for what a first paper on the task can claim: the first strong
number on MUVOD will be informative mainly in proportion to how carefully it is
taken apart.

The task is a question of spatial intelligence posed on real-world video
rather than on a reconstructed scene.
A rig video is the raw input of volumetric capture and of every per-object 3D
reconstruction or generation pipeline downstream of it, and those pipelines
consume multiview masks: one mask per object, camera, and frame is what a
segmented 3DGS or a per-object radiance field is trained from, and today a
person supplies it by annotating camera after camera. The question we ask is
whether a 2D segmentation foundation model, given the object once, maintains a
geometrically and temporally consistent notion of it across the rig's
viewpoints---without poses, without a 3D model, on scenes with moving people,
environment objects, and fisheye optics---and, where it does not, whether
explicit 3D reasoning (structure-from-motion, feed-forward point maps, or a
per-scene 3DGS) can supply what it lacks. Sections~\ref{sec:exp}
and~\ref{sec:budget} measure the first; Section~\ref{sec:negative} answers the
second, negatively, with a finding about 3DGS on sparse wide-baseline rigs that
bears on 3D reconstruction from real-world video beyond segmentation.

We therefore build the simplest pipeline that could plausibly work---the
benchmark's own two-stage structure with the video tracker replaced by a
promptable segmentation foundation model, SAM~3 \cite{sam3}---add three small
design elements, and then spend most of the paper measuring rather than
claiming. The pipeline reaches \OursBasic\ \JF\ on the \emph{basic} protocol
and \OursComplete\ on \emph{complete}, against \XMemBasic\ and \XMemComplete\
for the published XMem baseline (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). Reported as a single margin, that is \DeltaBasic\ points; \pctFoundation\% of
it, however, is the distance from the published baseline to the same two-stage
structure with the tracker replaced---which, since the baseline was not rerun
in our code, also carries implementation and evaluation differences---and
\gainOurs\ points come from our design, measured within our pipeline
(Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The
latter is small in absolute terms but removes \gainOursPctGap\% of the gap the
tracker replacement left. We estimate the optimism of its final arm selection
by leave-one-scene-out (\losoOmega\ points, Table~\ref{tab:loso}); the design
of the arms on the same scenes is not covered by that estimate.

The second half of the paper asks where the remaining \totalGap\ points are.
Rerunning Stage~2 from ground-truth seeds on the three scored cameras raises
the control configuration by \gtSeedControlDelta\ points and the full pipeline
by \gtSeedHeadlineDelta, to \gtSeedHeadline: the intervention recovers
\gtSeedHeadlinePct\% of the gap, and \gtSeedRemaining\ points remain after it
(Table~\ref{tab:budget}). Most of the initialization loss sits not in the
\nFzero\ nonreference seeds that fail outright but in the small imperfections of
the \nNzero\ that do not. A model-free measurement---ground truth passed
through the tracker's own low-resolution mask grid---shows that the grid alone
can account for at most \lossNow\ points (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). Finally we
report five preregistered attempts to repair the failing seeds by exemplar
detection, dense correspondence, and three geometry-lifting routes
(Table~\ref{tab:negative}). All five fail their registered gates; propagated
through Stage~2, a ground-truth-free vote over their candidates changes the
benchmark by \voteReal\ points (an interval that spans zero) and even the best
frame-0 choice among them gains only \oracleReal, a seed that is best at frame 0
not being best after tracking. The generators fail for want of correct
candidates; the vote fails for a structural reason: there are
\nFzero\ nonreference seeds to repair and \nNzero\ to protect. A closing
demonstration on a \plenCams-camera studio ring runs the same pipeline from a
single text prompt on one camera (Section~\ref{sec:plenoptic}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Segmentation on a Reconstructed 3D Representation}
SPIn-NeRF \cite{spinnerf} and SA3D \cite{sa3d} formalized ``one-view prompt
$\rightarrow$ all-view masks'' on top of a per-scene radiance field, and the
representation moved to 3DGS with Gaussian Grouping \cite{gaussiangrouping} and
its successors. The current generation lifts masks onto a \emph{pre-trained}
3DGS without further optimization---FlashSplat \cite{flashsplat}, LUDVIG
\cite{ludvig}, SAGOnline \cite{sagonline}---and has pushed NVOS mIoU to
about 92, where the latest entries are separated by tenths of a point
(SAGOnline reports $+0.1$ over SAGA \cite{sagonline}). Every member of this
family assumes COLMAP poses \cite{colmap} and a reconstruction of sufficient
quality; SANeRF-HQ lists the quality of the radiance field among its
limitations \cite{sanerfhq}. Section~\ref{sec:negative} tests exactly this bridge on the benchmark's pinhole
rigs of \gsViewsMin--\gsViewsMax\ cameras and finds that on every one of the
\gsCams\ cameras with a reference depth the rendered depth is biased toward
the camera, and that the error grows, rather than shrinks, with training-view peak
signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR).

\subsection{Views as a Video}
Ordering the cameras and propagating a first-frame mask with a video object
segmentation tracker is the structure of the MUVOD baseline \cite{muvod}, of
OSTRA \cite{ostra}, and of the pre-processing stage of Gaussian Grouping; the
memory-based trackers XMem \cite{xmem}, DEVA \cite{deva}, Cutie \cite{cutie},
and SAM~2 \cite{sam2} are the usual engines. WildSeg3D \cite{wildseg3d} pairs
the same SAM~2 propagation with a global alignment of MASt3R point maps
\cite{mast3r}. The approach inherits the
tracker's prior that consecutive frames are nearly aligned, and it degrades
accordingly at wide baselines \cite{segmast3r}. Our two-stage baseline belongs
to this family: its cross-view stage is a single
bidirectional pass of a SAM~3 tracker over the cameras' first frames in index
order. What we add on top (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is small, and we measure it
as small.

\subsection{Correspondence and Uncalibrated Promptable Methods}
Training-free feature correspondence---Matcher \cite{matcher}, PerSAM
\cite{persam}---turns a reference mask into prompts for a query image through
DINOv2 \cite{dinov2} or SAM \cite{sam} features; its sensitivity to viewpoint change is
documented \cite{probing3d}. Feed-forward geometry models
\cite{dust3r,mast3r,vggt,pi3} now provide poses and point maps without
calibration, and a 2026 family injects them into promptable segmenters:
MV-SAM \cite{mvsam}, 3AM \cite{am3}, and G$^{2}$TAM \cite{g2tam}, alongside
learned segment matching such as SegMASt3R \cite{segmast3r}. These are the
closest existing designs to our input format. None reports MUVOD, none
evaluates environment objects, and none has been run on fisheye rigs; that is
why they do not appear as columns in Table~\ref{tab:headline}, a limitation we
return to in Section~\ref{sec:limits}. We do, however, evaluate training-free
ingredients of the same kind as seed generators in Section~\ref{sec:negative}:
feed-forward $\pi^{3}$ point maps as used by MV-SAM and G$^{2}$TAM,
foundation-feature (DINOv2) correspondence in the manner of Matcher and PerSAM,
and SAM~3 exemplar detection.

\subsection{Rig Benchmarks}
MUVOD \cite{muvod} is, to our knowledge, the only public benchmark with
synchronised multicamera rigs, per-instance masks, and a fixed protocol. Its
3D benchmark, on which four posed NeRF and 3DGS methods are reported, excludes
the four fisheye scenes and one further scene whose reconstruction was
judged too poor, and its four methods all require camera poses; its video
object segmentation
benchmark, which we use, includes all \nScenes\ scenes, provides no
calibration, and carries a single published result. Its reference camera per scene is not published,
which we address in Section~\ref{sec:cini}.

\section{Problem Setting and Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Task}
A rig of $N$ synchronised cameras $c_{1},\dots,c_{N}$ records $T+1$ frames
each. One camera, the reference \cini, carries a set of binary object masks
$\{M_{o}\}$ at frame $t=0$. For every object $o$, every camera $c$, and every
frame $t\in\{0,\dots,T\}$ the method must output a mask $\hat{M}_{o,c,t}$.
Intrinsics and extrinsics are not provided and are not estimated for use in
the pipeline; no scene-specific training or optimization is performed; the
segmenter is a frozen pre-trained model. All frames of all cameras may be
read---the benchmark's own annotation procedure and its baseline both assume
this. Objects are propagated jointly: the tracker resolves overlaps between
them by score, and the seed-repair rules of Section~\ref{sec:negative} read the
other objects' seeds.

\subsection{The MUVOD Metric}
MUVOD scores three annotated cameras per scene: \cini\ and two others.
For each scored camera, region similarity \Jscore\ (intersection over union,
IoU) and boundary accuracy \Fscore\ are computed per object over the
\nFramesPerCam\ annotated frames with the DAVIS definitions \cite{davis2016}.
The per-camera score is the mean over objects, the scene score the mean over
the three cameras, and the benchmark score the mean over \nScenes\ scenes.
We write the result \JF. Two object sets are defined: \emph{basic} counts only
objects visible in the reference frame, \emph{complete} counts every labeled
object. The two coincide for a scene exactly when the reference frame already
contains all of that scene's labeled objects, a property we use below. Our
scoring code is the DAVIS implementation, unchanged. Two choices the benchmark
leaves open we fix conservatively: an object absent from a camera's ground
truth is not scored for that camera, since the alternative awards a perfect
score to an empty prediction, and all \nFramesPerCam\ frames are scored rather
than dropping the first and last as the DAVIS code does.

\subsection{Recovering the Reference Camera}
\label{sec:cini}
The benchmark describes \cini\ as a camera ``near the center of the rig'' but
does not name it, and the \emph{basic} object set depends on it. We determined
it per scene from three independent sources. (A) The distributed index records
the rig geometry; on linear, arc, and planar rigs the middle-numbered annotated
camera is the one nearest the geometric center. (B) For the six scenes whose camera numbering
is not spatial---four hemispherical rigs and two two-row planar rigs---we read
the original datasets' calibration files and chose the annotated camera closest
to the rig center in metric coordinates. (C) The published tables themselves
constrain the answer: in eight scenes the basic and complete scores are equal,
which means the reference frame contains every labeled object, and in three of
those scenes exactly one annotated camera satisfies that condition in the
ground truth. In those three scenes (C) agrees with (A) or (B). One scene, Dog,
remains ambiguous: (C) excludes one of its three annotated cameras, the
remaining two lie on the same ring of the hemisphere, and we adopt the one
marginally nearer the axis. Both candidates were run in two
earlier configurations of the pipeline, with and without cross-view memory and
before crop retracking; the adopted camera gives the lower Dog score in both
(\dogCini\ against \dogAltCiniA\ and \dogAltCiniB), so the reported figure is
conservative with respect to this ambiguity. The final configuration was not
rerun with the alternative. The choice matters: the reference rule of our own earlier
runs---the camera whose frame-0 ground truth carries the largest object index,
ties going to the first camera listed---selects a different camera from \cini\
in \nCiniMismatch\ of \nScenes\ scenes, typically one at the edge of the rig.

\subsection{A Two-Stage Baseline}
\label{sec:twostage}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the structure. \emph{Stage~1} arranges the first
frames of all $N$ cameras in camera-index order as a pseudo-video, gives the
reference masks $\{M_{o}\}$ as the only prompt on the frame of \cini, and runs
the SAM~3 video tracker once forward to the end of the sequence and once
backward to its start. The result is one seed mask $\hat{M}_{o,c,0}$ per
(camera, object). \emph{Stage~2} opens one temporal session per camera, prompts
it with that camera's seeds at $t=0$, and tracks forward to $t=T$. The segmenter
is SAM~3 \cite{sam3}, whose tracker inherits the memory-attention design of
SAM~2 \cite{sam2}: a bank of memory tokens from recent frames, each carrying a
learned temporal-position encoding, plus per-object pointer vectors. It runs at
a \modelInput-pixel input and predicts masks on a $\gridNow\times\gridNow$
low-resolution grid before upsampling, a fact that matters in
Section~\ref{sec:grid}.

This is structurally the MUVOD baseline. The benchmark authors describe theirs
as a straightforward two-stage baseline that applies XMem both spatially and
temporally, and note that, being natively mask-propagation-based, it cannot
fully exploit the multiview information. Ours is that structure
with the tracker exchanged. We verified that the control code path contains
nothing else by byte comparison: with cross-view memory disabled, Stage~2
writes \nBytePNG\ PNG masks on the 15 development scenes identical to those of
an independent single-view package that has no cross-view code at all.

\subsection{Cross-View Memory During Tracking}
\label{sec:xview}
In the baseline structure the cameras never communicate after Stage~1. We let
them communicate through the tracker's own memory: at frame $t$, camera $c$'s
memory bank additionally receives the memory token and object pointer of its
index neighbors within a window $W=\xviewWindow$. The neighbors are the
previous camera at frame $t$ and the next camera at frame $t-1$, so that each
read refers to a frame that has already been computed. Three conditions govern
the neighbor tokens. They pass the same quality gate as the camera's own
memory (a low-confidence neighbor frame is dropped exactly as a low-confidence
own frame would be); the neighbor's object pointer is appended after the
camera's own pointers; and the neighbor token is assigned the temporal-position
encoding of row \xviewRow, i.e., ``five frames ago,'' rather than row 0
(``one frame ago''). For reproducibility: cameras are processed in index
order; at either end of the index range the missing neighbor is skipped, with
no wrap-around; the gate is the tracker's own threshold on its effective IoU
score (0.01); neighbor pointers are appended after the camera's own; and only
cameras up to the highest scored index are run, which changes no scored output
(verified by byte comparison against running every camera).

The last choice is grounded in a measurement. Comparing pixel change between
adjacent cameras at the same instant with pixel change between the same camera
at $t$ and $t-k$, one camera step exceeds $k=\viewStepFrames$ frames of change
in 91\%--100\% of (camera, frame) pairs on each of the 15 development
scenes. The tracker's temporal encoding has seven rows, six of them
nonconditioning, so the neighbor is placed near the far end of them. The
curve over the shift is not monotonic: it peaks one row short of the end, at
row \xviewRow, and falls again at row 5. Window size saturates
immediately---on a six-scene subset of the development split, $W=2$, $4$ and
$6$ change the mean by at most $+0.0007$ over $W=1$---and the pointer and the
shift, each individually positive, and the gate, whose own effect is
indistinguishable from zero, overlap rather than add when combined.

Two earlier registered tests of this component did not pass; we report them. Its first preregistered test, at our original edge-of-rig reference
cameras, found no effect (paired $\Delta$ of a $W=4$ one-sided window over no
window, on the 20 nonreference cameras with at least four neighbors:
\firstTestDelta, 95\% confidence interval (CI) \firstTestCI). A direction
ablation likewise found no mode passing its registered criterion. Only after
the protocol moved to the benchmark's central \cini\ did a re-registered test
of the second, next-camera direction pass: relative to the one-sided variant,
the nine cameras that previously had no neighbor gained
\ciniTestDelta\ (CI \ciniTestCI), and the benchmark moved by \ciniTestScenes\
points with no scene falling below the registered floor. What changed between
the failing and the passing test was the number of cameras a neighbor could
reach and the direction it was read from, not the memory mechanism; the full
component's \gainXview\ over no cross-view memory has not itself passed a
preregistered test.

What the memory does when it acts is visible at the event level. We
registered a symmetric rule and applied it to both configurations: a
\emph{divergence interval} is a maximal run of at least three consecutive
frames in which one configuration loses an object ($\Jscore<0.1$) while the
other holds it ($\Jscore>0.5$) on at least two of those frames, searched in
both directions over every scored (camera, object) pair of the \nScenes\
scenes, with the memory turned on and off and nothing else changed (no crop
retracking in either arm). Three intervals exist, all in the direction of the
memory: one late entry (PoznanStreet, a pedestrian who walks into view at
$t=\xrPoznanEnter$) and two occlusions (Fencing, a foil lost when the blades
cross at $t=\xrFencingLost$; Breakfast), with a mean gain of
\xrGainPerInterval\ in \Jscore\ over the interval; no interval goes the other
way, and the same three recur when the reference camera is changed.
Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery} shows two of them. Three intervals are too few for a
statistical claim and we make none. The count says that divergences large
enough to meet the registered rule are rare in the 21-frame clips and that
where one occurs the gain is large; it does not measure how often the memory
changes a mask by less than the rule requires.

\subsection{Raw Frame-0 Seeding}
\label{sec:fs}
Stage~2 repredicts frame~0 from the seed it is prompted with, and the
reprediction is slightly worse than the seed. We write the seed itself as the
frame-0 output. For the reference camera the seed is the ground truth after one
pass through the tracker's mask encoding (a $\gridNow\times\gridNow$ round trip,
Section~\ref{sec:grid}), not the raw mask, which is why its frame-0 score in
Section~\ref{sec:budget} is \refJfZero\ rather than 1. The effect on the
benchmark is \gainFsSeed\ points.

\subsection{Zoom-Gated Crop Retracking}
\label{sec:crop}
Small objects lose most on the $\gridNow\times\gridNow$ grid. For every (camera, object)
whose seed covers fewer than $\cropAreaMax$ pixels we open a second, single-object
tracking session inside a square window centered on the seed's bounding box,
with side $s=\mathrm{clamp}(\cropScale\cdot\max(w,h),\ \cropMinSide,\
\min(H,W))$, resized to the model's \modelInput-pixel input; the effective zoom
is $\modelInput/s$. The session reads no neighbor memory and its result is
pasted back into the full-frame output for frames $1$ to $T$. The seed rule
uses the seed, not the ground truth, so it is available at inference. On the
reference camera, whose seeds are the ground-truth masks after the round trip
of Section~\ref{sec:fs}, the \nCropRefSmall\ objects
under $2\times10^{3}$\px\ rise from a mean \Jscore\ over frames 1--20 of
\cropRefSmallBefore\ to \cropRefSmallAfter, which is the mechanism working as
intended.

A fixed window also fails on its own terms: an object that moves quickly can
leave it, and a window that contains a similar object can capture the wrong
one. A ground-truth-free
gate reverts a pair to the full-frame result when any of three conditions
holds. The crop-tracked mask at frame~1 overlaps the seed with IoU below
\gateIoU; the zoom is below \gateZoom, so the crop gains no resolution; or the
object touches the window edge in \gateBorder\ or more of the 20 tracked frames.
Across the benchmark \nCropTargets\ pairs qualify and \nCropReverted\ are
reverted. The thresholds were chosen on the scored scenes; Section~\ref{sec:loso}
measures what that cost.

\subsection{Preregistration and Controls}
\label{sec:prereg}
Every confirmatory experiment in this paper was preregistered: its
hypothesis, population, acceptance rule, and predicted outcome were written and
committed to the repository before the run. Where a rule was made or
re-registered after data were seen we say so: the direction test
(Section~\ref{sec:xview}) and the crop gate, whose thresholds were fixed on the
scored scenes (Sections~\ref{sec:crop} and~\ref{sec:loso}). Exploratory
measurements---the window curve, the view-step measurement, runtime, and the
leave-one-scene-out analysis---were not registered. There are \nPrereg\
registration documents in the repository. The default code path is guarded by
\nTests\ CPU tests and by byte-level identity checks. Cameras that receive no
neighbor change by exactly zero; the \nByteNontarget\ masks of pairs not
touched by crop retracking are byte-identical to the previous configuration;
and the \nByteSeeds\ reference-camera seeds are byte-identical in the Stage-1
vote experiment of Section~\ref{sec:negative}. The code, the registration
documents, the seed sets, and the per-scene results are kept in a
version-controlled repository and are available from the corresponding author
on request.

\subsection{Decomposition of the Margin}
\label{sec:ladder}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} climbs from the published baseline to our result one
element at a time. The first step---from the published baseline to the same two-stage
structure with the tracker replaced---is worth \gainFoundation\ points,
\pctFoundation\% of the total. The three elements we designed contribute \gainXview, \gainFsSeed,
and \gainCrop, together \gainOurs. Two qualifications apply. First, the \gainFoundation\ step cannot be
split further into ``foundation model'' and ``two-stage structure'': we never
ran XMem inside our pipeline, so the structural equivalence with the benchmark
baseline is argued from the benchmark's description, not measured, and the
step also absorbs any difference in implementation and evaluation conditions
between the two codebases. Second, and
for the same reason, the converse claim---that any modern tracker would produce
this number---is equally unmeasured; to our knowledge no foundation-model
method has been evaluated on MUVOD, and at the time of our survey (September
2026) no paper citing the benchmark was indexed by Semantic Scholar or Google
Scholar. What is established is the size of our own design's share: \gainOurs\ points,
\gainOursPctGap\% of the \gapAfterTracker\ points that remained after the tracker
replacement.

\subsection{How Much of the Gain Is Selection Optimism?}
\label{sec:loso}
The crop area threshold and the gate variant of Section~\ref{sec:crop} were
chosen among six candidate arms by looking at all \nScenes\ scored scenes.
We estimate the resulting optimism by leave-one-scene-out: for each scene the
same selection rule---largest mean gain subject to no scene falling more than
\floorPts\ points---is reapplied to the other 16, and the chosen arm's gain is read
on the held-out scene. Table~\ref{tab:loso} gives the result. The fitted gain
is \losoFitted, the held-out gain \losoHeldOut, and their difference
$\Omega=\losoOmega$ points, \losoOmegaPct\% of the effect; \losoAgree\ of
\nScenes\ folds select the same arm. The estimate covers the selection among the six arms only: the arms
themselves---two area thresholds and three gate variants---were designed after
inspecting results on the same scenes, and that adaptive step is not covered;
nor is \losoOmega\ a statistical bound. Table~\ref{tab:loso} therefore lists the
six arms with their fitted gains and the registered floor each met or violated,
so that the reader can see what the selection had to choose from.

\subsection{Oracle Decomposition}
Table~\ref{tab:budget} collects every oracle we ran. The most informative rows
are the first two: replacing the frame-0 seed of the three scored cameras by
ground truth and rerunning the unchanged Stage~2 raises the control
configuration from \controlBasic\ to \gtSeedControl\ (\gtSeedControlDelta,
95\% CI \gtSeedControlCI, 17 of \nScenes\ scenes) and the full pipeline from
\OursBasic\ to \gtSeedHeadline\ (\gtSeedHeadlineDelta, CI \gtSeedHeadlineCI).
In the control configuration the cameras are tracked independently, so the gain is the effect of a correct seed
on each scored camera, \gtSeedControlPct\% of that configuration's
\controlGap-point gap, and the \gtSeedControlRemaining\ that remain are what
the tracker loses from a correct seed. In the full pipeline the unscored
neighbors that feed cross-view memory keep their Stage-1 seeds, so the gain is
the effect of the intervention on the three scored cameras,
\gtSeedHeadlinePct\% of the \totalGap\ points, and the \gtSeedRemaining\ that
remain are what is left after it, not the tracking error of a perfectly
initialized rig. Where the initialization loss sits is the finding. In the control
configuration the \nFzero\ failing nonreference pairs gain \gtSeedFzeroGain\ each
on average over frames 1--20 (\gtSeedFzeroRec\ recovered), but the \nNzero\ sound
pairs also gain \gtSeedNzeroGain\ each, \gtSeedNzeroUp\ of them by more than 0.05,
so the small imperfections of seeds that do not fail cost more in total than
the failures do. Table~\ref{tab:contrib} splits each gain over the scored pairs with the
benchmark's own weights: in both configurations the sound pairs contribute
more than the failing ones (\gtSeedNzeroContribCtrl\ against
\gtSeedFzeroContribCtrl\ of the \gtSeedControlDelta\ control points). Two objects of 2 and 6 px vanish in
the tracker's mask encoding and are the only pairs whose ground-truth seed is
not written back at frame 0. The output-replacement rows, which do not rerun
the tracker and overlap with one another, give the older and narrower reading:
repairing only the 15 pairs whose final frame-0 mask fails (\JFpair\ ${<}0.5$)
is worth \oracleSeed, a perfect reference camera \oracleRef, and perfect masks
on the \nOrdinaryPairs\ sound pairs \oracleOrdinary. Region \Jscore\ (\regionJ)
is worse than boundary \Fscore\ (\boundaryF), though the benchmark's boundary
tolerance of 0.8\% of the image diagonal (\boundaryTol\px\ across scenes)
makes \Fscore\ lenient. The two candidate-seed rows are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:negative}.

\subsection{A Deficit From the First Frame; Mean Drift Is Small}
The reference camera is prompted with ground truth, yet its per-frame \Jscore\
is \refJfZero\ at $t=0$ (the frame-0 output is the prompt after the tracker's
mask round trip, Section~\ref{sec:fs}), \refJfOne\ at $t=1$, and then flat
(\refJfFive, \refJfTen, \refJfTwenty\ at $t=5,10,20$). The drop occurs at the
first tracked frame and does not accumulate. The nonreference cameras are flat
from the start at a lower level: their \JFpair\ is \nonrefJFzero\ at $t=0$,
\nonrefJFten\ at $t=10$, and \nonrefJFtwenty\ at $t=20$, against \refJFzero,
\refJFten, and \refJFtwenty\ on the reference camera (Fig.~\ref{fig:perframe}).
A flat mean can hide compensating movements, so we also look per pair and per
object type. From $t=1$ to $t=20$ the median change per nonreference pair is
\driftMedianNonref\ (reference camera \driftMedianRef); \driftDownNonref\ of the
\nNonrefPairs\ nonreference pairs fall by more than 0.1 and \driftUpNonref\ rise
by as much; \driftFellNonref\ cross below 0.5 and \driftRoseNonref\ cross above
it. By object type, dynamic and environment objects lose on average
\driftDynNonref\ and \driftEnvNonref\ over the window and static objects
\driftStaNonref. Drift is real for a minority of moving objects, but in the
population it is an order of magnitude smaller than the deficit present at
$t=0$, which points at the seed and at per-frame mask quality rather than at
accumulation over time.

\subsection{The Mask Grid Is Not the Bottleneck}
\label{sec:grid}
The largest gain among our own design choices (\gainCrop,
Section~\ref{sec:crop}) came from a resolution device, which invites the
hypothesis that resolution is the dominant residual. We tested what the output grid alone can
cost, without a model: the ground-truth masks are downsampled to the tracker's
$\gridNow\times\gridNow$ grid, upsampled back, and scored against the originals
under the benchmark protocol (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). The round-tripped ground
truth is one mask that output space can represent, so the best representable
mask scores at least \ceilNow, and the loss attributable to the grid alone is at
most \lossNow\ of the \totalGap\ points; a $\gridMid\times\gridMid$ grid would
bound it at \lossMid\ and a grid at the model's input resolution
($\modelInput\times\modelInput$) at \lossHigh. The round-trip loss depends
on object size alone---\ceilSmall\ for the \nSmallRows\ pairs under
$2\times10^{3}$\px, \ceilMedium\ for the \nMediumRows\ between
$2\times10^{3}$ and $10^{4}$\px, \ceilLarge\ for the \nLargeRows\ above---and
not on rig type or camera role. The hypothesis is therefore rejected: the
preregistered reading of the \shareNow\% share is ``substantial but not
dominant'' (at the registered 20\% boundary), and at least \gapNotGrid\ of the
\totalGap\ points lie outside what the output grid can explain. Crop retracking
changes the input resolution and context as well as the effective grid, so its
gain is not a measurement of the same quantity. Because the round trip uses no
model, it is a property of the benchmark and applies to any tracker with the
same output grid.

\section{Five Seed-Repair Attempts and Where Each Fails Its Gate}
\label{sec:negative}

Replacing the outputs of the 15 pairs whose final frame-0 mask fails is worth
\oracleSeed\ points (Table~\ref{tab:budget}), and rerunning Stage~2 from the
best frame-0 candidate of the generators below \oracleReal. Neither is a
ceiling on seed repair---ground-truth seeds recover \gtSeedHeadlineDelta---but
both say the room in the outright failures and in the candidate set we tested
is small. The failing seeds are nevertheless the
most visible errors---an object placed on the wrong thing, or missing
altogether---and the literature of
Section~\ref{sec:related} offers several training-free ways to produce a better
one. We tested five, each preregistered with its acceptance gate, and report
all five.

\emph{G-geo-b} replaces the reconstruction by $\pi^{3}$ feed-forward point maps

None passes. The interesting part is \emph{how} each fails, because the
failures are not the same. G-det finds a correct candidate on \gateDet\ of
\nQualPairs\ failing pairs but also produces at least one candidate on
\detHalluc\ of the ground-truth-empty rows. G-corr works on sound pairs---a
correct candidate exists on \corrSoundExist\% of N0 with median IoU
\corrSoundIoU---and fails precisely where it is needed: on pinhole failing pairs
at view distance $\geq 4$ it finds \corrFarPinhole. G-geo-a is limited by
availability: SfM and dense stereo are available on \geoAavail\ of the
\nGtPresent\ rows with ground truth, failing outright on two scenes and losing
a required camera on two more, and where they are available the stereo depth
either has holes or returns background depth on the failing objects---moving
people, textureless walls, glass. G-geo-b removes the availability problem
and its depth is accurate---median relative error \geoBdepthErr\% against
multiview stereo where both exist---but its point-map resolution cannot resolve
the failing objects, and it passes \gateGeoB\ of \nGeoPairs.

G-gs deserves a paragraph because it is the bridge that the entire
3D-representation family of Section~\ref{sec:related} relies on. The
reconstructions are good by their own measure: training-view PSNR is
\gsPSNRmin--\gsPSNRmax~dB and the rendered depth covers the target objects.
Yet the median relative depth error against MVS ranges from \gsDepthBest\% on a scene whose
training-view PSNR is among the \emph{lowest} (31--36~dB) to \gsDepthWorst\%
on a scene among the highest (40--42~dB), and across the \gsCams\ cameras with
a reference depth the rendered surface is nearer than the stereo surface on
every one. With \gsViewsMin--\gsViewsMax\ wide-baseline views per scene,
semi-transparent Gaussians placed
anywhere along a ray reproduce the training images; PSNR rises and the surface
dissolves into floaters. A depth that is dense and wrong is worse than
one that is missing: the four pairs G-geo-a passed all collapse under G-gs, and
\gateGeoC\ of \nGeoPairs\ pass. The scope of this finding is our configuration:
3DGS trained on the first frames with the COLMAP poses for \gsIter\ iterations,
$\alpha$-normalized expected depth read from the renderer, and our lifting, on
\gsScenes\ pinhole scenes that have a COLMAP dense-stereo depth to compare
against (\gsCams\ cameras, in the same coordinate frame, so no scale alignment
is involved). The reference depth is itself an estimate; the error is the median
absolute relative difference where both depths exist, and the bias is the sign
of the median difference. The finding says nothing about 3DGS segmentation
methods that never read a depth.

\subsection{Candidate Availability and the Limits of SeedVote}
\label{sec:cands}
The union of all generators contains a correct candidate for \unionOracle\ of
the \nQualPairs\ qualifying failing pairs, so the information is present in
the candidate set. The same union produces a nonempty candidate on
\unionHalluc\ of the ground-truth-empty rows. Any selector must therefore
separate ``the object, elsewhere'' from ``something else, anywhere'' without
ground truth. We registered one, SeedVote. A candidate replaces the default
seed only if it is dissimilar to the default (IoU ${<}\voteTau$), agrees with
at least one other stream or with the geometric reprojection of the reference
mask (IoU ${\geq}\voteTau$), is closer than the default to the reference object
in SAM~3's object-pointer space, and does not overlap another object's seed. On
rows where the default seed is empty, a stricter agreement count is required so
that the hallucinating union cannot fill empty rows. The vote replaced \nVoteReplaced\ of \nGtPresent\ seeds. At the seed (frame 0)
it was accurate on the failing set: \voteFzeroRep\
replacements, \voteFzeroRec\ recovered, \voteFzeroBroke\ broken, paired
$\Delta\Jscore=\voteFzeroDJ$ (CI \voteFzeroCI). On the sound set it was not:
\voteNzeroRep\ replacements, \voteNzeroBroke\ of which broke a working pair,
$\Delta\Jscore=\voteNzeroDJ$ (CI \voteNzeroCI). Propagated through the unchanged Stage~2 (Table~\ref{tab:budget}), the vote
changes the benchmark by \voteReal\ points (CI \voteRealCI, \voteWins\ scenes
up and \voteLosses\ down): the interval spans zero, so the vote is not shown
to hurt, only to miss its registered gate. An oracle that picks, for every
row, the candidate with the highest frame-0 IoU against ground truth gains
\oracleReal\ (CI \oracleRealCI); this is the best \emph{frame-0} choice, not
the candidate whose propagated result is best, so it is not a ceiling on what
the candidate set could yield. The seed-level conversion we had registered as an optimistic
bound predicted \voteRule\ and \voteOracle: it was right about the vote and
\oracleOverestimate$\times$ too optimistic about the oracle, because a seed
that is best at frame 0 is not always best after propagation---the oracle's
replacements recover \oracleFzeroRec\ failing pairs but break
\oracleFzeroBroke\ others. Hallucination on the empty rows did not increase.
At the seed level the hemisphere rigs alone show a net gain with no new
failures ($\Delta\Jscore=\voteHemiDJ$, CI \voteHemiCI); restricting the rule to
them would be a post hoc choice, and we do not make it.

\subsection{The Structural Reason}
\label{sec:asymmetry}
The result generalizes beyond our rule, as a condition rather than a verdict,
under a stated simplification. Partition the candidates for replacement into
$n_F$ failing and $n_N$ sound pairs, weight every pair equally, and summarize
a selector by four numbers: the fraction $r$ of failing pairs it repairs and
the net gain $g$ per repaired pair, every other change on the failing pairs
being folded into $g$; and the fraction $b$ of sound pairs it breaks and the
net loss $\ell$ per broken pair, improvements to sound pairs being folded into
$\ell$, which they reduce. The net change is then proportional to
$n_F\,r\,g-n_N\,b\,\ell$ and is a gain only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:breakeven}
\frac{b}{r} < \frac{n_F\, g}{n_N\, \ell}.
\end{equation}
The exact benchmark change is
$\Delta B=\sum_i w_i\,(S_i^{\mathrm{new}}-S_i^{\mathrm{old}})$ over all scored
pairs, with $S_i$ the pair's time-averaged score and $w_i$ the weight the
object--camera--scene aggregation gives it; \eqref{eq:breakeven} is the
equal-weight, two-population reduction of that sum, and we evaluate it at the
seed frame in \Jscore, whereas the benchmark scores the propagated
video---Table~\ref{tab:budget} shows that the two can differ. On this
benchmark $n_F=\nFzero$ and $n_N=\nNzero$, and the vote's own seed-level
outcomes give $g\approx\gainPerRecovered$ and $\ell\approx\lossPerBroken$ (in
\Jscore\ per pair), so the right-hand side is about \breakEvenRatio: at these
gains and losses a selector must damage sound seeds roughly \breakEvenTimes\
times less often than it repairs failing ones. SeedVote repaired
\voteRepairRate\ of the failing pairs and damaged \voteDamageRate\ of the sound
ones, a ratio of \voteBR, and did not pass; the two earlier attempts in this
project---a post hoc seed repair and exemplar detection---failed the same
inequality. Propagated and weighted as the benchmark weights them
(Table~\ref{tab:contrib}), the vote's \voteReal\ points are \voteFzeroContrib\
from the failing pairs and \voteNzeroContrib\ from the sound ones: the damage
on the majority cancels the repair on the minority. The frame-0 oracle, by
contrast, gains on both populations (\oracleFzeroContrib\ and
\oracleNzeroContrib), so its shortfall against the seed-level prediction is not
the asymmetry but the frame-0/propagated mismatch of Section~\ref{sec:cands}. The factor of \breakEvenTimes\ is the condition at
SeedVote's own $g$ and $\ell$, not a constant of the benchmark; what the
inequality makes explicit is that on a rig benchmark the damage rate on the
majority population, not recall on the failing minority, decides the sign, and
we suggest that future work on seed repair report both rates.

\section{Beyond the Benchmark: A 32-Camera Studio Ring}

MUVOD scores three cameras per scene and seeds every method from a
ground-truth mask. To see the pipeline on a dense rig, and without any mask at
all, we ran it on one scene from a plenoptic studio: \plenCams\ synchronized 4K
cameras on a ring (about \plenRingDeg$^{\circ}$ apart) around six people who
circle a flag holding hands, so that in every camera each person passes behind
others. The scene is our own capture and is not public; this is a
demonstration, not a benchmark. We use \plenFrames\ frames at \plenFps\
frames/s (\plenSeconds\ s), downsampled to \plenRes, and hand-drawn polygon
masks of the six people on the first frame of every camera (\plenPairs\
nonreference (camera, person) pairs carry ground truth; one person is
unannotated in each of two cameras). No ground truth exists after the first
frame. The protocol was preregistered; the reference camera is the one whose
annotation shows all six people with the largest total mask area---a protocol
choice made from the annotations, the model receiving only that camera's
masks or prompt---and cameras are indexed in ring order so that index
neighbors are physical neighbors.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We did not rerun the XMem baseline. Its numbers are transcribed from
        the benchmark paper; the evaluation conditions were matched to the
        release where checkable (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), but the equivalence
        of the two pipelines' structure is argued from the paper's description,
        not measured.
  \item There is one baseline and one dataset. SAM~2, Cutie, DEVA, and the
        2026 uncalibrated promptable methods do not appear as columns because,
        to our knowledge, none has been evaluated on MUVOD under its protocol
        (our own SAM~2 runs are diagnostic and predate the protocol); running
        them is a contribution in itself and outside the present scope.
  \item Cross-view memory (\gainXview) failed its first two preregistered
        tests---an on/off test at our edge-of-rig reference cameras and a
        direction ablation---and passed a third, re-registered one at the
        benchmark's reference camera. The mechanism is the same; the reader
        should weigh the component accordingly.
  \item The crop gate was selected on the scored scenes. Section~\ref{sec:loso}
        estimates the optimism of the choice among the six arms at \losoOmega\
        points; the design of the arms on the same scenes is not covered.
  \item MUVOD scores three cameras per scene, so the Stage-1 populations of
        Section~\ref{sec:negative} live on two nonreference cameras per scene.
  \item The four fisheye scenes are scored but excluded from the
        geometry arms, and from MUVOD's own 3D benchmark; the geometry
        conclusions are for pinhole rigs.
  \item The reference camera of one scene (Dog) is determined up to two
        candidates on the same ring of the rig; the adopted one scored lower in
        the two earlier configurations where both were run
        (Section~\ref{sec:cini}), and the final configuration was not rerun
        with the other.
  \item The ground-truth-seed oracle replaces the seeds of the three scored
        cameras only; in the full pipeline the unscored neighbors that feed
        cross-view memory keep their Stage-1 seeds, so its gain in the full
        pipeline is the effect of that intervention, not of perfect
        initialization of the whole rig, and we do not claim it as a bound in
        either direction. In the control configuration the cameras are tracked
        independently and the caveat does not arise.
  \item The studio-ring demonstration is one scene of our own capture, not
        public, with ground truth on the first frame only; its divergence
        events number one in the 5-s clip and one pair in the 10-s clip.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
On the only public rig benchmark we know of, a two-stage pipeline with a promptable
foundation model improves on the published baseline by \DeltaBasic\ points,
of which \gainOurs\ is ours. Of the remaining \totalGap\ points, rerunning the tracker from ground-truth
seeds on the scored cameras recovers \gtSeedHeadlineDelta, \gtSeedHeadlinePct\%
of the gap; most of that initialization loss sits in seeds that do not fail
outright, and neither the output grid nor temporal drift explains much of what
remains. The visible failures---seeds placed on the wrong object---are where
repair was attempted: the five preregistered candidate generators fail their
gates for want of correct candidates, and where candidates exist the one
preregistered ground-truth-free rule over their union does not demonstrate a
net gain (\voteReal, an interval spanning zero), because what it breaks among the \nNzero\ sound
pairs cancels what it repairs among the \nFzero\ failing ones; even the best
frame-0 choice among the same candidates gains only \oracleReal\ once
propagated, because a seed that is best at frame 0 is not always best after
tracking. The \asymmetry\ ratio, and the break-even condition it implies at a
selector's own gains and losses, are what the next selector for this task
should be designed against. For video-driven spatial intelligence the message is
twofold: a 2D segmentation foundation model already carries most of the
cross-view consistency a real-world rig demands, and on rigs of
\gsViewsMin--\gsViewsMax\ wide-baseline cameras the explicit 3D routes we
tested---structure-from-motion, feed-forward point maps, and per-scene
3DGS---do not yet add to it. On a dense studio ring, a text prompt on one
camera started it at a first-frame mean \Jscore\ of \plenJtxt\ on the
other cameras against \plenJgt\ from ground-truth seeding.
